Chronicle

Organizations & Machines

The apparatus between a leader and the people they rule — urban machines, dominant parties, cadre movements, palace guards and patronage networks. Each is read the same way: who it mobilised, what it traded, the chain of brokers from the top to the street, how it kept people in line, where the money came from and how it ended. An organization outlives its leaders, so it gets a page of its own.

Convergence

Seven independent ways of reading a leader — Rubenzer’s traits, Simonton’s styles, Hermann’s trait analysis, the carrots and sticks they reached for, the selectorate they answered to, Svolik’s account of how they held an autocracy, and how their power ended. Where they agree, the portrait is probably right. Where they contradict each other, either a framework is wrong about that leader or the leader was genuinely two things at once — and that is where the real thinking is.

Temperament

Three psychologies of leadership. Rubenzer, Faschingbauer & Ones measure personality traits — nine NEO-PI-R facets that predict presidential greatness, scored as percentiles against U.S. presidents, a reference class already extraordinary. Simonton measures leadership styles — five behavioural factors describing what a leader actually does in office. Hermann reads seven traits from what leaders say off-script and combines them into eight styles of handling a situation. Two facets are reverse-coded because the asset is the absence of the thing: Not Vulnerable and Not Straightforward. Bands are real scholarly disagreement, not decoration.

Political Time

Stephen Skowronek’s question is not who the leader was but where they stood: affiliated with the governing order they inherited, or opposed to it — and was that order still resilient, or already failing? The answers give four recurring positions, each with its own opportunity and its own way of going wrong. It is the one lens in the atlas that measures the situation rather than the person, which is why the ablest leader in the index can sit in the worst chair.

Survival

Two questions every ruler without an independent enforcer has to answer, from Milan Svolik’s The Politics of Authoritarian Rule: how to share power with the few who could remove you, and how to control everyone else — by repression, which empowers the armed men who do it, or by co-optation, which is expensive. Bruce Bueno de Mesquita asks who a leader actually has to keep happy — the winning coalition inside the selectorate — and what they must be paid. Mancur Olson’s bandits ask how rulers taxed and how long they expected to stay. Then, for every leader in the atlas, the Archigos questions: how did they come to power, and what happened to them after it ended?

Patronage

The machines in the catalogue were built on patronage; this page asks how patronage actually works now. Sixteen twenty-first-century systems, from Peronist brokers in Buenos Aires to Orbán’s procurement state, read through the in-depth studies — ethnographies, field experiments, surveys, commissions of inquiry — that have overturned much of the common wisdom. Much of what looks like vote buying is turnout, signalling or audience-building, enforcement is rarely surveillance, and development has tended to move patronage from the voter to the contract rather than end it.

Practices

The blocking and tackling: not who they were but what they did every day — how many calls, which notes, what they did with the afternoon, whose company they kept. Ari Emanuel credits some two hundred calls a day; Churchill rehearsed his rhetoric obsessively. These pages ask the same of political leaders, separate what they credited themselves from what others observed, grade the evidence, and say what each habit cost. Worked examples so far — Johnson, Clinton and James Baker, the operator who never held elected office — and the template for the rest.

Carrots & Sticks

The instruments of rule, filed by what they are for: trust, loyalty, fear, motivation. Each tool carries its characteristic failure mode — the cost column is usually the useful one. Creeds sets what leaders said moved people against what they actually reached for.
